Abstract.Housing societies in the Mumbai Metropolitan Region re-
ceive heavy monsoon rainfall yet buy water from private tankers in the
dry season. Rooftop rainwater harvesting (RWH) can help, but com-
mon committee-level planning tools assume all annual rainfall is usable,
ignore storage limits and rarely justify their recommendations. This pa-
per presents WaterWallet, a zero-hardware, web-based decision-support
tool that estimates how much water a housing society can save. It es-
timates demand, measures rooftop catchment on a map, retrieves his-
torical rainfall, and simulates storage-constrained savings. A daily water
balance, with a fixed eligible non-potable demand of 65 litres per capita
per day, drives tank sizing and configuration comparison. Determinis-
tic return on investment (ROI) uses an annual-yield upper bound, and
a 1,000-scenario Monte Carlo analysis samples monthly rainfall; neither
is storage-constrained. A deterministic hydrogeological safety gate pre-
cedes a calibrated Random Forest configuration classifier. In a controlled
single-storm test, a 10,000 L tank delivered 6,840 L of useful water and a
100 L tank 1,100 L under identical inflow. An optional SARIMA rainfall
forecaster showed no consistent advantage over the average rainfall of
each calendar month. The machine-learning components are prototypes
trained on rule- or formula-generated labels, so their metrics do not es-
tablish real-world accuracy. WaterWallet suits academic demonstration
and preliminary feasibility screening; operational use requires measured
meter, rainfall, harvest, cost and groundwater data.

1 Introduction
Rapid urbanization has strained the municipal water supply of the Mumbai
Metropolitan Region. In dense areas such as Thane, Kalyan and Navi Mumbai,
cooperative housing societies routinely buy tanker water to top up the municipal
supply, which creates recurring costs that rise with price inflation. Nationally, a

---PAGE 2---
2 N. Shirur et al.
large share of the population faces high water stress [15], and the Central Ground
Water Board reports groundwater shortages in many urban regions [4].
Rooftop rainwater harvesting (RWH) is encouraged in the region: municipal
rules require it on large plots with increasing enforcement [6], and a cooperative
society in Bengaluru reports saving around INR 3 lakh per year for a relatively
small investment [5]. Yet individual residential buildings adopt it slowly, for three
reasons. Committees get no site-specific guidance on whether to use storage,
borewell recharge or both; financial models rest on a single annual rainfall value
that ignores year-on-year variability; and tools rarely explain why a particular
system is recommended. Monsoon rain also falls in a few large storms, and water
beyond tank capacity on a storm day is lost. Tools that treat every annual litre as
usable are therefore inaccurate, and planning must rely on daily water balances.
WaterWallet addresses these gaps through a browser-based system. The con-
tributions of this paper are:
1. an end-to-end, three-phase planning workflow (Virtual Meter, Map Studio,
ROI Dashboard) that requires no installed hardware;
2. a daily storage balance shared by tank sizing and configuration comparison,
together with a demand policy that separates the 65 LPCD eligible non-
potable target from 135 LPCD total planning demand;
3. a safety-first, explainable recommendation path in which a deterministic
hydrogeological gate takes precedence over a calibrated Random Forest clas-
sifier with TreeSHAP attributions; and
4. an explicit separation between physics-based calculations, which drive all
financial output, and prototype ML components, whose reported metrics
are scoped to the labels they were trained against.
The paper does not claim field-validated yield prediction, hydrogeological ap-
proval, potable-water design, or construction-ready costing.
2 Related Work and Background
Rainfall data, forecasting and water balance.Gauge records are rarely
available at housing-society scale, so gridded reanalysis products such as Open-
Meteo’s historical service [16] are a practical alternative, despite documented
spatiallimitations.Formonthlyforecasting,seasonalARIMA[1]withinformation-
criterion order selection [9] is reproducible, but for strongly seasonal monsoon
rainfall a plain calendar-month average is itself a demanding baseline, so Wa-
terWallet benchmarks its optional forecaster against it. Time-stepped storage
simulation is the standard way to account for finite tank volume, since useful
water is limited by what the tank can carry between days; WaterWallet applies
a daily balance to tank sizing and configuration comparison.
Machine learning and uncertainty.Random forests [2] suit small tabu-
lar problems, raw scores need sigmoid calibration [14], and TreeSHAP [12,11]
attributes predictions to features (model-relative explanations, not causal mea-
surements). Monte Carlo simulation [13] yields a payback distribution.

4.4 Rainfall Forecasting
The forecasting service requires at least 36 consecutive monthly observations
and fits an AIC-selected seasonal ARIMA with period 12 by stepwise search [9]
(deployed bounds: non-seasonal orders up to 3, seasonal orders up to 2, total
order up to 8). It returns non-negative 12-month forecasts with 95% intervals,
and rejects missing, non-finite, negative or insufficient input rather than replac-
ing it. Forecast rainfall is converted to yield with the same runoff and usability
factors; if fitting fails, the frontend keeps the historical-average result. Forecasts
are informative scenarios and do not drive the financial model. The benchmark
in Section 5.3 compared a wider search (orders up to 5) with an earlier tighter
one (orders up to 2 and 1); the wider search performed markedly better.
4.5 Tier 1: Hydrogeological Safety Gate
Before any classifier runs, a deterministic gate evaluates physical and regulatory
constraints on groundwater recharge. A triggered check forces Storage-Only with
confidence 1.0 and a plain-language reason (Fig. 2):

4.6 Tier 2: Calibrated Random Forest Classifier
Whenthegatepasses,aclassifierselectsamongStorage-Only,Borewell-Recharge
andHybrid(storagewithoverflowroutedtorecharge).ItisaCalibratedClassifierCV
(sigmoid, 5-fold) wrapping a 200-tree Random Forest over seven inputs: rooftop
area, borewell presence and depth, soil permeability class (1–5), coastal distance,
annual rainfall and peak-day rainfall. No labelled RWH configuration dataset ex-
ists for Indian housing societies, so the training script generates 600 synthetic
site profiles labelled by engineering rules derived from municipal guidance [6]
and the CPHEEO manual [7], split 80/20 and stratified (480/120). The classi-
fier therefore recovers its labelling rules and cannot discover geology absent from
them. TreeSHAP [12,11] gives per-feature contributions, and a plain-language
summary names the strongest positive drivers.
4.7 Prototype Yield Regressor
A 200-tree Random Forest regressor estimates monthly harvest from 10 inputs
(rooftop area, month, monthly and annual rainfall, a peak-day proxy, three
lagged monthly totals, and sine/cosine month encodings). It is trained on 10
rooftop sizes (200–1,500 m2) and 180 months (2010–2024), giving 1,800 rows
split chronologically into 1,440 training (2010–2021) and 360 test rows (2022–
2024).Itstargetisthephysicalcatchmentequation,soitapproximatesaformula.
It is shown as a diagnostic, never alters the physics-based financial yield, and
the service falls back to the physical equation if its artifact is absent.

4.9 Monte Carlo ROI
For each ofn= 1,000scenarios, monthly rainfall is drawn independently for
each calendar month. When site monthly rainfall from Phase 2 is available,
each month is drawn uniformly within±20%of the site value; otherwise it
is drawn uniformly between the P10 and P90 values of a historical percentile
table. If neither source is available, a built-in Thane-like distribution is used
and the response is flaggedis_site_specific = false. Monthly yield, limited
by the tank capacity and demand, feeds the savings and cash-flow equations of
Section 4.8. The engine returns the break-even probability by year, the median
and 10/50/90 break-even years, and the share of scenarios that never break even
within 10 years.
Because months are sampled independently, within-year rainfall correlation
and storm timing are lost, so the spread of outcomes may be misstated, and the
analysis does not use the daily balance of Section 4.3. Results are conditional on
the sampling ranges and the assumed costs and demand; they are not forecast
probabilities calibrated against observed project outcomes. Resampling complete
historical daily years through the daily balance is planned future work.

and does not affect the financial results.

6 Discussion and Limitations
Findings.Useful water is bounded by both demand and tank capacity, so more
rainfall does not guarantee more savings, and the same annual rainfall can yield
very different usable volumes depending on storm timing and tank size (Sec-
tion 5.2). This is the main argument against annual-total calculators in monsoon
climates. The two ROI analyses use different simplifications: deterministic ROI
is an upper bound that ignores storage, and Monte Carlo samples monthly rain-
fall without a daily balance, so neither reproduces the storage-constrained values
of the tank sizer. Historical municipal and tanker bills from the Thane society
were Phase 1 inputs establishing baseline water spend and the tanker-offset vol-
ume. No tank-meter reading, measured harvest or construction quotation was
collected, so yield, CAPEX and payback figures are model outputs under stated
assumptions, not validated against observed outcomes. Illustrative modelled sce-
narios are listed in the Appendix.
Limitations.
1.Data.Open-Meteo is gridded reanalysis, not an on-site gauge; gutter loss,
shading, intensity-dependent runoff, contamination and pump energy are
uncalibrated, and the first-flush factor is coarse.
2.ML labels.Classifier labels are rule-generated and the regressor is trained
on formula targets, so their metrics do not validate real-world accuracy; the
single Thane case cannot establish generalisation.
3.Forecaster.Over 2022–2025 it only matches a plain seasonal mean and is
sensitive to search bounds; forecasts are scenario previews and do not enter
the financial calculations.
4.ROI methods.Deterministic ROI assumes an ideal seasonal buffer and
MonteCarlosamplesmonthsindependently,soneitherisstorage-constrained
and the Monte Carlo spread ignores within-year rainfall correlation. The
Monte Carlo percentile table and fallback are Thane-specific.
5.Financial scope.The calculator excludes financing, tax, civil constraints,
electricity, replacement, water-quality treatment and recharge permissions;

6.Readiness and security.WaterWallet is a prototype: only society routes

end-to-end testing is absent, and the tool does not replace structural, hydro-
geological, water-quality or regulatory assessment.
7 Conclusion and Future Work
WaterWallet demonstrates a hardware-free workflow from society inputs and
map-based catchment estimates to storage-constrained tank sizing and financial
scenarios. Its key design decision is to treat rainfall as daily inflows rather than
an annual credit in tank sizing and configuration comparison. A deterministic
safety gate keeps physical constraints ahead of learned recommendations, and
ML claims are limited to rule- and formula-derived prototypes. The next mile-
stone is a monitored pilot with observed rainfall, tank levels, demand, bills and
quality data and independently reviewed site suitability. Further work includes
(i) making deterministic and Monte Carlo ROI use the daily balance, with Monte
Carlo resampling whole historical daily years [8] to preserve storm timing; (ii)
real labelled sites to replace synthetic labels, with held-out evaluation; (iii) a
multi-site test of whether a seasonal-mean forecaster should replace SARIMA;
(iv) a groundwater-recovery model for borewell recharge; (v) event-based first-
flush and intensity-dependent runoff; and (vi) consistent API authorisation, end-
to-end tests and monitoring.
