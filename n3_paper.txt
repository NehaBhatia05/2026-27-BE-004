Abstract.Housing societies in the Mumbai Metropolitan Region re-
ceive heavy monsoon rainfall yet buy water from private tankers in the
dry season. Rooftop rainwater harvesting (RWH) can help, but common
committee-level planning tools assume all annual rainfall is usable, ig-
nore storage limits and rarely justify their recommendations. This paper
presents WaterWallet, a zero-hardware, web-based decision-support tool
that estimates how much water a housing society can save. It estimates
demand, measures rooftop catchment on a map, retrieves historical rain-
fall, and simulates savings under storage constraints. One daily water
balance, with a fixed eligible non-potable demand of 65 litres per capita
per day, drives tank sizing, site-specific return on investment (ROI) and
a Monte Carlo analysis that resamples historical daily rainfall years. A
deterministic hydrogeological safety gate precedes a calibrated Random
Forest configuration classifier. In a controlled single-storm test, a 10,000
L tank delivered 6,840 L of useful water and a 100 L tank 1,100 L under
identical inflow. An optional SARIMA rainfall forecaster showed no con-
sistent advantage over the average rainfall of each calendar month. The
machine-learning components are prototypes trained on rule- or formula-
generated labels, so their metrics do not establish real-world accuracy.
WaterWallet suits academic demonstration and preliminary feasibility
screening; operational use requires measured meter, rainfall, harvest, cost
and groundwater data.

1 Introduction
Rapid urbanization has strained the municipal water supply of the Mumbai
Metropolitan Region. In dense areas such as Thane, Kalyan and Navi Mumbai,

cooperative housing societies routinely buy tanker water to top up the municipal
supply, which creates recurring costs that rise with price inflation. Nationally, a
large share of the population faces high water stress [15], and the Central Ground
Water Board reports groundwater shortages in many urban regions [4].
Rooftop rainwater harvesting (RWH) is encouraged in the region: municipal
rules require it on large plots with increasing enforcement [6], and a cooperative
society in Bengaluru reports saving around INR 3 lakh per year for a relatively
small investment [5]. Yet individual residential buildings adopt it slowly, for three
reasons. Committees get no site-specific guidance on whether to use storage,
borewell recharge or both; financial models rest on a single annual rainfall value
that ignores year-on-year variability; and tools rarely explain why a particular
system is recommended. Monsoon rain also falls in a few large storms, and water
beyond tank capacity on a storm day is lost. Tools that treat every annual litre as
usable are therefore inaccurate, and planning must rely on daily water balances.
WaterWallet addresses these gaps through a browser-based system. The con-
tributions of this paper are:
1. an end-to-end, three-phase planning workflow (Virtual Meter, Map Studio,
ROI Dashboard) that requires no installed hardware;
2. one canonical daily storage balance shared by tank sizing, deterministic ROI,
and Monte Carlo analysis, together with a demand policy that separates the
65 LPCD eligible non-potable target from 135 LPCD total planning demand;
3. a safety-first, explainable recommendation path in which a deterministic
hydrogeological gate takes precedence over a calibrated Random Forest clas-
sifier with TreeSHAP attributions; and
4. an explicit separation between physics-based calculations, which drive all
financial output, and prototype ML components, whose reported metrics
are scoped to the labels they were trained against.
The paper doesnotclaim field-validated yield prediction, hydrogeological ap-
proval, potable-water design, or construction-ready costing.
2 Related Work and Background
Rainfall data, forecasting and water balance.Gauge records are rarely
available at housing-society scale, so gridded reanalysis products such as
Open-Meteo’s historical service [16] are a practical alternative, despite docu-
mented spatial limitations. For monthly forecasting, seasonal ARIMA [1] with
information-criterion order selection [9] is reproducible, but for strongly seasonal
monsoon rainfall a plain calendar-month average is itself a demanding baseline,
so WaterWallet benchmarks its optional forecaster against it. Time-stepped
storage simulation is the standard way to account for finite tank volume, since
useful water is limited by what the tank can carry between days; WaterWallet
applies one daily balance to both sizing and finance.
Machine learning and uncertainty.Random forests [2] suit small tab-
ular problems, raw scores need sigmoid calibration [14], and TreeSHAP [12,

11] attributes predictions to features (model-relative explanations, not causal
measurements). Monte Carlo simulation [13] yields a payback distribution, and
resampling whole historical years [8] preserves storm timing that independent
monthly sampling destroys.
Gap.Municipal calculators typically use static annual rainfall and no storage
model, and sensor platforms need installed hardware. WaterWallet targets pre-
feasibility with no hardware, daily storage physics, a safety gate before any
learned recommendation, and explicit uncertainty.
3 System Architecture
The application pairs a React 19 and Vite frontend with a FastAPI backend
over JSON/HTTP (Pydantic v2 validation; PostgreSQL persistence through
SQLAlchemy and JWT authentication where configured); the core calculation
services are stateless per request. External inputs are the Open-Meteo archive
(daily precipitation), Esri World Imagery map tiles, and an optional large-
language-model advisory service that only rephrases already-computed metrics.

4.4 Rainfall Forecasting
The forecasting service requires at least 36 consecutive monthly observations
and fits an AIC-selected seasonal ARIMA with period 12 by stepwise search [9]
(non-seasonal orders up to 5, seasonal up to 2). It returns non-negative 12-
month forecasts with 95% intervals, and rejects missing, non-finite, negative or
insufficient input rather than replacing it. Forecast rainfall is converted to yield
with the same runoff and usability factors; if fitting fails, the frontend keeps the
historical-average result. Forecasts are informative scenarios and do not drive
the financial model. The search bounds were widened after evaluation, because
an earlier, tighter setting (orders up to 2 and 1) performed markedly worse
(Section 5.3).

4.6 Tier 2: Calibrated Random Forest Classifier
When the gate passes, a classifier selects amongStorage-Only,Borewell-
RechargeandHybrid(storage with overflow routed to recharge). It is a
CalibratedClassifierCV(sigmoid, 5-fold) wrapping a 200-tree Random Forest
over seven inputs: rooftop area, borewell presence and depth, soil permeability
class (1–5), coastal distance, annual rainfall and peak-day rainfall. No labelled
RWH configuration dataset exists for Indian housing societies, so the training
script generates 600 synthetic site profiles labelled by engineering rules derived
from municipal guidance [6] and the CPHEEO manual [7], split 80/20 and strat-
ified (480/120). The classifier therefore recovers its labelling rules and cannot
discover geology absent from them. TreeSHAP [12,11] gives per-feature contri-
butions, and a plain-language summary names the strongest positive drivers.

split chronologically into 1,440 training (2010–2021) and 360 test rows (2022–

6 Discussion and Limitations
Findings.Useful water is bounded by both demand and tank capacity, so
more rainfall does not guarantee more savings, and the same annual rainfall
can yield very different usable volumes depending on storm timing and tank
size (Section 5.2). This is the main argument against annual-total calculators in
monsoon climates. Deterministic ROI (average of historical years) and Monte
Carlo (resampled, perturbed years) share the same balance and should differ
only as a baseline versus a distribution. Historical municipal and tanker bills
from the Thane society were Phase 1 inputs establishing baseline water spend
and the tanker-offset volume. No tank-meter reading, measured harvest or
construction quotation was collected, so yield, CAPEX and payback figures
are model outputs under stated assumptions, not validated against observed
outcomes. Illustrative modelled scenarios are listed in the Appendix.
Limitations.
1.Data.Open-Meteo is gridded reanalysis, not an on-site gauge; gutter loss,
shading, intensity-dependent runoff, contamination and pump energy are
uncalibrated, and the first-flush factor is coarse.
2.ML labels.Classifier labels are rule-generated and the regressor is trained
on formula targets, so their metrics do not validate real-world accuracy; the
single Thane case cannot establish generalisation.
3.Forecaster.Over 2022–2025 it only matches a plain seasonal mean and is
sensitive to search bounds; forecasts are scenario previews and do not enter
the financial calculations.
4.Financial scope.The calculator excludes financing, tax, civil constraints,
electricity, replacement, water-quality treatment and recharge permissions;

conservative.
5.Readiness.WaterWallet is a prototype: API authorisation is not applied uni-
formly across endpoints (a security gap to close before deployment), end-to-
end testing is incomplete, and it does not replace structural, hydrogeological,
water-quality or regulatory assessment.
7 Conclusion and Future Work
WaterWallet demonstrates a hardware-free workflow from society inputs and
map-based catchment estimates to storage-constrained yield, tank selection and
financial scenarios. Its key design decision is to treat rainfall as daily inflows
rather than an annual credit, so storage, demand, overflow and carry-over are
shared by tank sizing, deterministic ROI and bootstrap Monte Carlo analysis. A
deterministic safety gate keeps physical constraints ahead of learned recommen-
dations, and ML claims are limited to rule- and formula-derived prototypes. The
next milestone is a monitored pilot with observed rainfall, tank levels, demand,
bills and quality data and independently reviewed site suitability. Further work
includes (i) real labelled sites to replace synthetic labels, with held-out evalua-
tion; (ii) a multi-site test of whether a seasonal-mean forecaster should replace
SARIMA; (iii) a groundwater-recovery model for borewell recharge; (iv) event-
based first-flush and intensity-dependent runoff; and (v) consistent API autho-
risation, end-to-end tests and monitoring.
