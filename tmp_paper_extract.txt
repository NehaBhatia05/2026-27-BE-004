Abstract—Housing societies in the Mumbai Metropolitan Re-
gion (MMR) receive intense monsoon rainfall yet buy private
tanker water through the dry months. Rooftop rainwater harvest-
ing (RWH) is a recognised remedy, but committee-level planning
tools commonly credit annual rainfall as if all of it were usable,
ignore finite storage, and give no rationale for their recom-
mendations. This paper presentsWaterWallet, a zero-hardware,
web-based decision-support platform that carries a society from
demand estimation, through map-based rooftop measurement
and historical rainfall retrieval, to a storage-constrained yield
simulation, a safety-gated configuration recommendation, and de-
terministic and probabilistic return-on-investment (ROI) analysis.
Its central engineering element is a single daily water balance,
shared by tank sizing, site-specific ROI, and a Monte Carlo engine
that bootstraps complete historical daily rainfall years. A fixed
eligible non-potable demand policy of 65 litres per capita per day
(LPCD) is separated from the 135 LPCD total planning load. A
deterministic hydrogeological safety gate precedes a calibrated
Random Forest classifier, whose predictions are explained with
TreeSHAP. The implementation was verified with 28 backend
tests (plus 5 parameterised subtests), 56 frontend tests, and a
controlled capacity-sensitivity experiment in which a 10,000 L
tank delivered 6,840 L from a one-storm synthetic year while a
100 L tank delivered 1,100 L under identical inflow and demand.
An optional Auto-SARIMA rainfall forecaster was benchmarked
against a plain calendar-month average on 2022–2025 Thane
data and showed no consistent advantage, so it is offered only
as a scenario preview. The machine-learning components are
reported as prototypes: their offline metrics are measured against
rule- or formula-generated labels and are not evidence of field
accuracy. WaterWallet is suitable for academic demonstration
and preliminary feasibility screening; operational use requires
observed meter, rainfall, yield, cost, and groundwater data.
Index Terms—rainwater harvesting, decision-support system,
daily water balance, tank sizing, Monte Carlo simulation, ex-
plainable machine learning, SHAP, Auto-SARIMA, Mumbai
Metropolitan Region.
I. INTRODUCTION
Rapid urbanisation has stressed municipal water distribution
across the Mumbai Metropolitan Region. Cooperative housing
societies in dense districts such as Thane, Kalyan and Navi
Mumbai commonly supplement municipal allocations with
private tankers, so water cost is both recurring and exposed
to price inflation. National assessments place a large share of
India’s population under high water stress [1], and the Central
Ground Water Board has documented groundwater depletion
in many urban regions [2].
Rooftop rainwater harvesting is policy-endorsed in the re-
gion: municipal regulations require RWH in larger plots, and
enforcement has tightened over time [3]. Documented deploy-
ments in comparable cities, such as a cooperative society in
Bengaluru reporting annual savings of roughly INR 3 lakh on a
modest investment, show the financial case is real [4]. Yet stan-
dalone residential adoption remains limited. Three planning
barriers recur: (i) committees lack site-specific guidance on
whether storage, borewell recharge, or a hybrid is appropriate;
(ii) financial projections rest on a single annual rainfall figure
that hides inter-annual and intra-annual variability and the
physical limit imposed by tank capacity; and (iii) tools rarely
explainwhythey recommend a configuration.
The storage limit deserves emphasis. Monsoon rain arrives
in a few intense events. A tool that credits every annual litre
as usable overstates savings, because water that exceeds tank
capacity on a storm day overflows rather than serving later
demand. Planning must therefore be done on adailywater
balance rather than on annual or monthly totals.
WaterWallet addresses these barriers with a browser-based
workflow. This paper makes four contributions:
1) an end-to-end, three-phase planning workflow (Virtual
Meter, Map Studio, ROI Dashboard) that requires no
installed hardware;
2) one canonical daily storage balance shared by tank
sizing, deterministic ROI, and Monte Carlo analysis, to-
gether with a demand policy that separates the 65 LPCD
eligible non-potable target from 135 LPCD total plan-
ning demand;
3) a safety-first, explainable recommendation path in which
a deterministic hydrogeological gate takes precedence
over a calibrated Random Forest classifier with Tree-
SHAP attributions; and
4) an explicit separation between physics-based calcula-
tions, which drive all financial output, and prototype ML
components, whose reported metrics are scoped to the
labels they were trained against.
The paper doesnotclaim field-validated yield prediction, hy-
drogeological approval, potable-water design, or construction-
ready costing.\n\n---PAGE 2---\nII. RELATEDWORK ANDBACKGROUND
Rainfall data and forecasting.Gauge records are rarely
available at housing-society scale, so gridded reanalysis prod-
ucts such as Open-Meteo’s historical service [5] are a practical
alternative, with documented spatial limitations. For monthly
forecasting, seasonal ARIMA [6] with information-criterion
order selection [7] is a reproducible choice, but for strongly
seasonal monsoon rainfall a plain calendar-month average is
itself a demanding baseline, so WaterWallet benchmarks its
optional forecaster against it.
Water balance and storage.Time-stepped storage simu-
lation is the standard way to account for finite tank volume:
useful water is limited by what the tank can carry between
days. WaterWallet applies one daily balance consistently to
sizing and finance.
Tree ensembles and explanation.Random forests [8]
suit small tabular problems, raw scores need sigmoid cali-
bration [9], and TreeSHAP [10], [11] attributes predictions
to features; these are model-relative explanations, not causal
measurements.
Uncertainty.Monte Carlo simulation [12] yields a payback
distribution; resampling whole historical years [13] preserves
storm timing that independent monthly sampling destroys.
Gap.Municipal calculators typically use static annual rain-
fall and no storage model, and sensor platforms need installed
hardware. WaterWallet targets pre-feasibility: no hardware,
daily storage physics, a safety gate before any learned rec-
ommendation, and explicit uncertainty.
III. SYSTEMARCHITECTURE
The application is a React 19 and Vite frontend commu-
nicating over JSON/HTTP with a FastAPI backend. Request
payloads are validated with Pydantic v2. PostgreSQL persis-
tence through SQLAlchemy and Alembic, together with JWT-
based authentication, supports society, billing and analysis
records where configured; the core calculation services are
stateless per request. External inputs are the Open-Meteo
archive (daily precipitation), Esri World Imagery tiles for the
map, and an optional large-language-model advisory service
that only rephrases already-computed metrics. Fig. 1 sum-
marises the flow.
A. Phase 1: Virtual Meter
The user enters flats, occupants per flat and monthly billing
rows (municipal bill, tanker count, tanker capacity, cost per
tanker). Tanker volume is the product of tanker count and
capacity, summed per row. Missing municipal or borewell
volumes in the monthly water audit are preserved asunknown
rather than silently treated as zero.
B. Phase 2: Map Studio
The user traces the roof on satellite imagery. Turf.js com-
putes geodesic polygon area on WGS84 coordinates, and the
polygon centroid is used to retrieve a rolling 15-complete-
calendar-year daily rainfall series from Open-Meteo. The same
screen collects site parameters (borewell presence and depth,
soil permeability class, coastal distance) for the configuration
recommendation.
C. Phase 3: ROI Dashboard
The dashboard requests itemised CAPEX and OPEX, a
deterministic multi-year ROI, a Monte Carlo break-even distri-
bution, candidate tank sizes, and a configuration comparison.
Cost defaults (per-area collection cost, per-litre storage cost,
and 3% annual maintenance) are editable planning assump-
tions, not vendor quotations.

the strongest positive drivers.
G. Prototype Yield Regressor
A 200-tree Random Forest regressor estimates monthly
harvest from 10 inputs: rooftop area, month, monthly rainfall,
annual rainfall, a peak-day proxy (monthly rainfall/30), the
three preceding monthly rainfall totals, and sine/cosine month
encodings. It is trained on 10 rooftop sizes (200–1,500 m 2)
and 180 months (2010–2024), giving 1,800 rows split chrono-
logically into 1,440 training (2010–2021) and 360 test rows
(2022–2024). Its target is the physical catchment equation, so
it approximates a formula. It is shown as a diagnostic and
neveralters the physics-based financial yield; if its artifact is
absent the service falls back to the physical equation.

VI. EVALUATION
Evaluation used the checked-out implementation and its test
suites [17]. We separate three kinds of evidence: software
verification, a controlled physical experiment, and offline ML
artifact metrics. Classifier and regressor metrics below are
artifact metrics read from saved model metadata against rule-
or formula-generated labels; they do not validate real-world
accuracy (classifier: accuracy, balanced accuracy and macro-
F1 all 1.000 on 120 held-out generated labels; regressor: MAE
4.8973 kL,R 2 = 0.9865on 2022–2024 formula targets).
A. Software Verification
The executed commands were the backend tests for the
Monte Carlo integration, tank sizer and ROI engine, and the
frontend test and build commands. The backend run passed
28 tests and 5 parameterised subtests; the frontend run passed
56 tests across 12 files; the production build completed. A full
endpoint test run did not complete during the audit window

C. Rainfall Forecasting Benchmark
Data.Daily Open-Meteo precipitation for Thane (19.22 ◦N,
72.97◦E), 1 January 2010 to 31 December 2025, was validated
(5,844 records, no gaps, duplicates or negative values) and
aggregated to 192 monthly totals; mean annual rainfall is
1,822.9 mm, concentrated in the southwest monsoon (Fig. 3).
The benchmark uses all 16 available years, whereas the
deployed application retrieves the most recent 15 complete
calendar years for each user’s site (Section III).
Protocols.Two chronological protocols avoid leakage; ran-
dom splits are not used. InProtocol A(single origin), models
are fitted on January 2010–December 2021 (144 months)
and forecast all 48 months of 2022–2025 with no new
observations. InProtocol B(rolling origin), every model is
refitted each January on all earlier data and forecasts the
next 12 months, for four folds (2022–2025) whose forecasts
are pooled. Baselines are chosen to be legitimate under each
protocol: aseasonal mean(training average of each calendar
month) and asame-month-last-yearbaseline (fixed at the last
observed year in Protocol A, genuinely rolling in Protocol B).
A recursive Random Forest (lags 1 and 12, 3-month mean,
sine/cosine month) feeds only its own predictions back as lags.
An earlier comparison used a same-month-last-year baseline
that read observed holdout values; it is not like-for-like with
a 48-month blind forecast, and its result is not used here.
Results.Table II reports pooled errors. Three findings stand
out. First, the plain seasonal mean is as good as or better
than every model on MAE under both protocols. Second, the
deployed wide-search Auto-SARIMA matches it in Protocol
A (MAE 63.76 vs. 62.72 mm, with slightly better RMSE
andR 2) but is about 4 mm worse in Protocol B (66.17 vs.
62.40 mm); there is no consistent advantage in either direction.
Third, the earlier tighter search (orders up to 2 and 1) was
much worse (MAE 156.13 mm in Protocol A, 81.69 mm
in Protocol B), which shows strong sensitivity to the search
bounds and motivated the wider setting now deployed. The
recursive Random Forest underperforms both, consistent with
error accumulation on short, zero-inflated series. The highR 2
values mostly reflect the repeating seasonal cycle, which even
the plain average captures (R 2 ≈0.80), so they should not be
read as forecasting skill.
Interpretation.With only 48 months, four folds and one
location, the differences between the seasonal mean and the
wide-search SARIMA are small and not statistically estab-
lished; a formal test such as Diebold–Mariano would have very
little power on 48 strongly autocorrelated, overlapping-horizon
errors and is therefore not reported. The defensible conclusion
is that the forecaster adds no demonstrated accuracy over a
plain monthly average. It is therefore offered as an optional
scenario preview. This does not affect the financial results,
which use observed historical daily rainfall years rather than
forecasts.

VII. DISCUSSION ANDLIMITATIONS
Findings.Because useful water is bounded by both demand
and tank capacity, more rainfall does not guarantee more
savings, and the same annual rainfall can yield very different
usable volumes depending on storm timing and tank size
(Section VI-B); this is the main argument against annual-total
calculators in monsoon climates. Deterministic ROI (average
of historical years) and Monte Carlo (resampled, perturbed
years) share the same balance and should differ only as
a baseline versus a distribution. Fixing eligible demand at
65 LPCD, rather than the buffered planning load, keeps the
denominator auditable. Historical municipal and tanker bills
from the Thane society were entered as Phase 1 inputs to
establish baseline water spend and the tanker-offset volume
used in the ROI model. However, no tank-meter reading,
measured harvest or construction quotation was collected, so
the resulting yield, CAPEX and payback figures are model
outputs under stated assumptions and are not validated against
observed project outcomes.
Limitations.
1)Data.Open-Meteo is gridded reanalysis, not an on-site
gauge; gutter loss, shading, intensity-dependent runoff,
contamination and pump energy are uncalibrated, and
the first-flush factor is coarse.
2)ML labels.Classifier labels are rule-generated and the
yield regressor is trained on formula targets, so their
metrics do not validate real-world accuracy. The single
Thane case cannot establish regional generalisation; 30–
50 or more reviewed sites are needed.
3)Forecaster.Over 2022–2025 it only matches a plain
seasonal mean and is sensitive to search bounds; with
one location, four folds and 48 months the differences
are not statistically established. Forecasts are scenario
previews and do not enter the financial calculations.
4)Financial scope.The calculator excludes financing,
tax, civil constraints, electricity, replacement sched-
ules, water-quality treatment and recharge permissions;
recharge overflow is not credited as tanker replacement,
so hybrid ROI is conservative.
5)Readiness.WaterWallet is a prototype: API authorisation
is not applied uniformly across endpoints, which is a
security gap to close before any deployment, and end-to-
end testing is incomplete. It does not replace structural,
hydrogeological, water-quality or regulatory assessment.
VIII. CONCLUSION ANDFUTUREWORK
WaterWallet demonstrates a coherent, hardware-free work-
flow from society inputs and map-based catchment estimates
to storage-constrained yield, tank selection and financial sce-
narios. Its key design decision is to treat rainfall as a sequence
of daily inflows rather than an annual credit, so that storage,
demand, overflow and carry-over are shared by tank sizing,
deterministic ROI and bootstrap Monte Carlo analysis. A
deterministic safety gate keeps physical constraints ahead of
learned recommendations, and the paper limits ML claims to
rule- and formula-derived prototypes.
The next credible milestone is a monitored pilot with
observed rainfall, tank levels, demand, bills and quality data,
coupled with independently reviewed site suitability. Further
work includes (i) collecting real labelled sites and replacing
synthetic labels, with geographically and temporally held-
out evaluation and calibration reporting; (ii) a multi-site
